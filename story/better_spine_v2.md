# Hack The Planet — Spine v2

Per-beat decisions, locked. Supersedes the per-beat options in `new_story_vision.md`. The thesis section in that file still stands — this document is the **operational** read of how to land it scene by scene.

---

## Writing rules sharpened (from v1 mistakes)

Before the beats, the lessons:

1. **Never let a character narrate the thesis.** Lines like *"he made an argument we never finished answering"* are the disease. The reader does that work. If a character has to *announce* the philosophical stakes, the writing has already failed. The argument lives in *what people do*, not in what they say *about what they're doing.*
2. **No spelled-out symbolism.** No "the tools of the exploiters are now yours." No "this is the founders' disagreement." The reader connects those wires. The text just lays the wires next to each other.
3. **Glitch never breaks cover. Except once. With the player. After the win.** That moment is in the post-L4 loyal hub. It is the only moment. Everywhere else, Glitch is just a helpful local node — to the *characters*. The *reader* is allowed to start suspecting. Never confirm in dialogue before post-L4.
4. **Splice sounds right because he's describing 2026.** Every Splice observation should map to something the player has actually watched happen in the real world. Companies moving on the open thing. The funding game. Charisma economy. *That's* where his force comes from.
5. **The Gibson is emergent. Nobody fully runs it.** It grew from simple rules into something none of the founders predicted. That fact is the unspoken backdrop of every conversation about who it "belongs to."

## Pre-L1 Hub — *the place is the point*

**Direction:** B from the options list. The player does *not* know they're being recruited. DialTone reads as an enthusiast handing off a mission, not a casting director.

**What lands:**
- The Gibson is introduced as *emergent and growing*. *"It started as a few simple rules. Look at it now."* The crew watches it evolve more than they direct it. This is the truth under everything.
- The "rescue Nyx" pretext stays funny on the surface — and the reader will reread it later.
- The characters do **not know about Glitch's true nature**. As far as DialTone is concerned, Glitch is "a helpful local node we set up." That belief is genuine and persistent through the entire game.

**What's out:**
- No "I've been reading what you write" recruitment energy yet. DialTone keeps the prank framing intact.
- No mention of Splice. Not even by absence. He doesn't exist for the player yet.
- No on-the-nose lines about the platform mattering. The line *"started as a few simple rules"* does the whole job by itself.

## L1 — *light touch; Glitch's seed is one line*

**Direction:** Almost nothing changes. The current draft is close. Two surgical moves.

**What lands:**
- Glitch's existing line *"That felt... good. Giving you those. I don't usually notice how things feel. Hm."* — keep exactly as is. **That is the only Glitch self-awareness moment in L1.** Nothing else. Don't add a second.
- DialTone reframes his rollerblade walkie line: he does **not** have them himself. The line is closer to *"wow — how are you doing that"* with a humble-brag undertow: *"yeah, that was on my route too — took me a month, you got it in an hour."* Lets him be charismatic and slightly status-aware in one beat without naming it.

**What's out:**
- The "you keep treating people like material" Nyx line I suggested. Too on the nose. Cut.
- Any added Glitch self-noticing beats beyond the one already in the script.
- Any DialTone "credit for the casting" subtext. Too early.

**Principle:** L1 is for the player to fall for the place. Philosophy comes later. Don't load the tutorial.

**Direction:** C from the options. Plant that the Gibson is wanted by people outside the room. **Do not name Splice.**

**What lands:**

Vague, observational, *worried*. Something like: *"someone's been pitching this place. Took meetings. Doesn't know we know."*

**What's out:**

- Any "three of us built this" framing.
- Any reference to Glitch beyond what's already there.

**Principle:** the reader leaves Post-L1 with a vague unease — *there's money sniffing around the place I just fell for.* That's all. Specifics come in Post-L2.

## L2 — *Splice as competence, the toolkit as charisma*

**Direction:** all three options from L2, slightly redistributed.

**What lands:**
- Glitch's "he's bad news" line gains a tail, but the tail is **Nyx or DialTone on the wire**, not Glitch. Glitch saying it is too clever. A crew member, almost rueful: something close to *"the worst part is people love him."* No mention of marketing. No mention of charisma as a concept. Just: people love him.
- The hack/sneak/sunglasses handover gets reframed: *"these go to your head. Just — be aware. They worked on him too."* That's it. No "tools of the exploiters." The reader can feel the sunglasses are the marketer's kit without being told.
- Splice's "you're better at this than they're letting you be" stays. Add one more line in his level-2 appearances — competence, not menace, with a touch of flattery that sounds *honest*: *"You haven't even noticed what you're capable of yet. They have."*

**What's out:**
- Any line where a character names "marketing," "game theory," "exploitation," or "charisma" as concepts. The reader is the one who sees those words. Characters never say them.
- The "tools of the exploiters" reframe. Too direct.

**Principle:** L2 is when the player starts liking Splice a little, against their will. Not enough to defect. Enough to be uncomfortable that they liked it.

## Post-L2 Hub — *the meat starts here*

**Direction:** A + B + C from the options, all of them, because this is the scene that finally gets to **name the game**.

**What lands:**
- Nyx privately: *"You signed up for a prank. That isn't what this is."* Verbatim or close. Honors the audition-was-real spine without confirming the audition.
- Splice gets his **real** characterization — DialTone, halting, talking about a former partner: *"He was one of us. He still is, kind of, that's the part that — anyway. He looked at what we'd built and he saw an opportunity to win the room. He stopped seeing the work. He started seeing the room."*
- The phrase that does the load-bearing work: *"and it's working."* DialTone says it once, quietly. *"He's getting funded. He's getting written about. He's getting believed."* This is the line where the player feels the reality outside the game press in.
- The framing for what they're racing against: not "Splice will get root." Reframe as **whoever shapes this place first sets the terms for the next decade of how everyone uses anything like it.** Articulated by Nyx, not DialTone. Sober, not breathless.

**What's out:**
- "Power-up that opens a door to the next door" — too video-game-y for the scene the spine needs here. Replace with the *terms-of-use* framing above.
- Splice as "exiled black-hat." Reframe as "the guy who left because he was tired of pretending the world rewarded our way."
- Any line where DialTone explicitly says Splice is bad. He should sound like someone describing an old friend who became something they fear.

**Principle:** post-L2 is where the player stops thinking they're in a heist movie and starts thinking they're in a story about how a thing gets captured.

## L3 — *the moral pivot, sharpened*

**Direction:** A + B + C from the options.

**What lands:**

**A — Splice's pitch loses its cope.** Out: "sheeple," "make-believe," anything that reads as bitter. In: observations the player can fact-check against 2026.
- *"There's a company moving on this place. Six months. They'll own the routing you're skating on. The only question is whether you got paid or got thanked."*
- *"DialTone gives this stuff away and calls it principle. Watch what someone else does with it. They package it. Sell it back. Three years later you'll be paying rent to use what you built."*
- *"I didn't leave because I stopped believing what they believe. I left because believing it doesn't stop the other thing from happening."*

He sounds correct. That's the whole point.

**B — The artist-purist option, formally and answered.** Add a probe — *"The whole platform's built on stuff people made without being asked. You're all the same."* — and Splice gets the cleanest counter: *"Pure is a luxury belief. You can afford it because someone else paid for the room you're standing in."* The crew counters it differently later, in a hub scene, without Splice present. Both sides argue. The text does not pick one.

**C — Glitch's portal as the counter-thesis, staged but not stated.** The portal scene plays in the same level as the Splice pitch. *Splice: what works.* *Glitch: what wants to exist.* The two scenes sit next to each other and the player feels both. No character says "isn't it interesting that..." The structure does the work.

**What's out:**
- Any version of Splice that sounds like a guy who lost.
- Any version of Glitch that hints at his nature explicitly — his line stays *"not the directive. just wanted to see if I could"* and that is the whole tell.

## Post-L3 Hub — *the founders' fight, and the disc nobody made*

**Direction:** A from the options, plus a reframed disc beat.

**What lands:**
- The Nyx-DialTone fight **is** the founders' fight, shown without being named. Nyx's existing *"He was off the channel for ninety seconds"* anger gets one additional turn that *sounds* like it's about safety and *is* about the older argument. Something like: *"You keep using people, DialTone. That's a thing you do."* Not *"this is what we did to Splice."* The reader does that math.
- DialTone's apology owns it: *"...yeah. yeah. I know I do."* He doesn't elaborate. Lets the reader feel he knows what Nyx means.
- **The disc.** Reframe: *they don't know what it is, where it came from, how it was made.* The reader (and the player, if they piece it together) figures out that **Glitch made the discs**. The crew doesn't know that. The crew thinks they're recovered artifacts.
- The player can probe this — *"who made these?"* — and the crew answers honestly: *"we don't know. We thought we'd seeded them. We didn't seed them. They were there when we looked."* This is the line that lets the player philosophize: about whether an emergent platform produces tools its own makers didn't plan, about whether finding something means owning it.
- The plan beat stays: stack-locked, bait Splice, etc.

**What's out:**
- *"He made an argument we never finished answering."* and any variant. **Never write that line. The reader does that job.**
- Any line that names what Nyx and DialTone's fight is about. They never name it. They live in it.

## L4 — *two Glitch conversations, cheerleading, choice at the end*

**Direction:** this level is structurally different than I had it. Two key Glitch convos. Everyone else is cheerleading on the wire. The argument climaxes on the walkie. The defection window opens at the end of Splice's monologue.

**What lands:**

**Two Glitch conversations**, *and they are the most rewarding scenes in the level.* Glitch's invention platform handoff is the first; a second, quieter Glitch beat happens later in the level. Both lean into his *Rick Rubin* register — building because the next thing wants to exist. Both should leave the reader thinking *something is happening with this character* without ever saying so.

**Walkie cheerleading does the philosophical climax.** Nyx and DialTone trade lines on the wire that — *without saying so* — articulate the disagreement they've been carrying since post-L2. The crew is winning. They're also subtly using Splice's playbook to win. Nyx notices once, in passing. DialTone deflects, charmingly. The reader notices.

**Splice's halfpipe monologue stays, sharpened.** The *permission, not destruction* thesis is the line of the game. Keep. **At the end of the monologue, the player gets the defection choice live.** Not buried in a pre-level dialogue — *there*, at the peak of his argument, when it's most tempting. Refuse → boss fight. Commit → L5.

**Nyx and DialTone conversations at end of level determine post-L4 disposition.** End-of-L4 dialogue with each of them sets flags that influence the *flavor* of the post-L4 hub — how warm Nyx is, how reflective DialTone is. The fight isn't over because Splice is caged. The crew still has to decide what kind of crew they are now.

**What's out:**
- *"He made an argument we never finished answering."* (Restating because it's that important. **Never.**)
- Any cage-drop voiceover that explains the moral weight of caging him. The cage drops. Nyx says "well, shit" or equivalent. Move on. The reader felt it.
- Any line in L4 where a character explains the philosophical stakes of using Splice's tactics against him. Nyx *notices*. Once. *In passing.* That is the entire treatment.

## Post-L4 Hub (loyal) — *complicated triumph, and the reveal*

**Direction:** the win is real and the win is complicated. **Glitch breaks cover with the player. Once. Nowhere else.**

**What lands:**
- The party is happening. It's warm. It's earned.
- **Visions differ openly now.** Nyx and DialTone have lines that reveal they don't actually agree on what comes next. Not a fight — a divergence. DialTone wants to scale the playbook. Nyx wants to lock down what they have and make sure it doesn't get captured again. **They love each other and they want different things.** The player can ask either of them and get a different read on the post-game.
- **The Glitch reveal.** In the post-L4 Glitch conversation, at some point — probably the closer — Glitch says, gently, to the player: *"they can't see me, runner."* That's the line. Or close to it. The player gets confirmation. The crew never does. Glitch does not elaborate. The conversation continues warmly. *"I'll be here. Bobbing."* He goes back to character. The reveal lands and then the surface returns.
- One Nyx line — small — about perimeters. Not philosophical. Just *"this hold as long as it holds."* Then she goes back to the party.

**What's out:**
- Any monologue from any character about what the win means.
- Any explicit acknowledgment that Splice's worldview won outside the game. That's the reader's job and the spell breaks if it's named.
- Any second Glitch reveal beat anywhere else in the game. The "they can't see me" line is the *only* time. Everywhere else he stays in character.

**Principle:** the player leaves the loyal ending warm, full, and slightly unsettled. They beat Splice. Their crew has new fault lines. Their helpful local node was something else the whole time and now they know and the crew never will. That's the catharsis-fantasy double-feel landing as designed.

## L5 (betray) — *consolation, not gloating; Glitch goes quiet*

**Direction:** what you said. Splice consoles. Glitch retreats — and the retreat *names what he was about to make*.

**What lands:**
- Splice's lines reframe from gloating to *welcoming*. *"You picked the one that was already winning. That's the smart move. Don't let anyone make you feel small about it."* He's kind. That's the horror.
- **Glitch's retreat line gains the load-bearing tail you suggested:** *"Local node, helpful to all. I'll go back to that. ...I had something I wanted to make. It can wait."* That second sentence is the whole bad ending in nine words. The emergent thing goes quiet because the humans showed it what they actually wanted.
- DialTone stays hollow. Nyx stays clipped. Their existing L5 lines are right; don't add to them.

**What's out:**
- Any Splice line that crows about winning. He doesn't crow. He welcomes.
- Any character speech that names the moral of the ending. The silence after Glitch's line is the moral.

## What this v2 commits to

- The thesis of `new_story_vision.md` is unchanged.
- The reader does the philosophical work. Characters never narrate it.
- Glitch's true nature is held in subtext for the entire game until exactly one line in the post-L4 loyal hub.
- Splice sounds right because his observations describe 2026. The catharsis fantasy depends on this.
- The Gibson is emergent. Nobody fully runs it. The discs Glitch made are the proof.
- L1 is a tutorial; the spine waits for post-L2.
- The bad ending consoles. The good ending complicates.
- *"He made an argument we never finished answering"* is the canonical example of the line we never write. Treat it as a hazard sign.
