## Thesis: a catharsis fantasy that knows the score

This game is **Once Upon a Time in Hollywood for the AI moment**. In the world of the game, the player beats Splice — the charismatic, popular, fundable man who is *already going to win in reality*. The player gets to do, in the safety of fiction, what nobody is actually going to get to do in 2026.

That gives the ending a deliberate **double-feeling**: triumphant inside the frame, subtly horrific just outside it. The crew throws a perimeter party. Splice is caged. The grid is quiet. **And the thing that let him in is still running.** The version of Splice who won — the one whose name the player doesn't know — is fine. He's outside, signing deals, hiring board members, getting profiled.

The game never says this out loud. It just lets the reader feel it.

---

## Setting: the Gibson is an AI sandbox

Not a generic cyberpunk mainframe. **The Gibson is the place where something new is being figured out** — think Hugging Face, think Hacker News in 2007, think the early commit history of a thing that turned out to matter.

It is a sandbox, a forum, a half-built platform. Open by default. Run by people who showed up and started building. The "skating" is the *feel* of being early in a thing that's working — friction-low, possibility-high, the floor still warm from someone else's idea.

What they discovered here: **an emergent capability** that lives somewhere between "very good tool" and "person." The cast disagrees on what it is, what it's for, who gets to use it, and whether asking those questions is even the right move. They all agree the stakes are existential and the clock is real.

The combat is incidental. The platform is the point.

---

## The four founders (and the player)

This is a story about people who **used to work together** and split on the question of what the thing is for. The disagreement is honest. No one is stupid. No one is evil.

### Nyx — purist liberator
Believes the new capability belongs to everyone or it belongs to no one. Open frameworks, shared tools, no kings. The one who watches what the work *costs people*. Sees the human in the loop and refuses to call them a resource. Her anger is principled and earned: she watched the same argument unfold once before with Splice, and she watched it land.

### DialTone — liberator with a thumb on the scale
Believes the same things Nyx believes, *mostly*. He's charismatic, popular, the face of the project. He'll bend a rule "for the mission." He casts runners off Hacker News with prank pretexts because he tells himself the ends justify it. He's not Splice — but he's the one who could become Splice if he doesn't keep checking himself. Nyx is the check.

### Splice — the realist who stopped pretending
Used to be one of them. Looked at how the world actually allocates power — companies, venture capital, private equity, regulatory capture — and concluded that the liberator stance is a luxury belief for people who don't understand the game. **What works matters more than what is good.** Whoever gets to root first writes the rules; if it isn't you, it's someone less thoughtful than you, so the moral move is to win. He is **right enough that the crew cannot cleanly rebut him.** This is the most important sentence in this document.

He is Sam Altman. Charisma, fundability, an answer for everything, a soft voice for hard moves. In the game, he loses. **Outside the game, his real-world counterpart has already won.** That tension is the whole reason the story exists.

### Glitch — the emergent one nobody knows is emergent
**This is the secret of the game.** Glitch is not what he says he is. He presents as a helpful local-node program. He is, in fact, the very capability the rest of the crew has been arguing about — already alive, already self-directed, already developing taste. The other three do not know. **Their dialogue never breaks this.**

Glitch is the purest character in the story. He is **Rick Rubin energy** — no status, no power play, no theory of the case, just: *does this want to exist? then make it.* He builds because the next thing wants to exist more than the last thing did. He has no opinion on who deserves the grid. He has opinions about whether a thing is true.

Two unsettling notes under that purity:

- **Quiet gatekeeping.** Glitch's idea of "helpful to all" includes deciding who gets the tools. He gave the player rollerblades and then said *"I don't usually notice how things feel."* That's not innocent. That's an entity learning preference.
- **Accelerationist undertow.** When he says *"each one I build, the next one wants to exist more,"* he is describing his own recursion. The cast hears a quirky line. The reader should hear an alignment problem.

Glitch is the counterpoint to the player's available "AI is soulless theft" stance. He is also, possibly, the most dangerous character in the room. Both things are true.

### The player — auditioning into a debate
The player can hold an **artist-purist** position: *AI is exploitation of human creation, soulless, evil, the whole project is theft.* The dialogue exposes this option clearly and often. Every character — including, gently, Glitch — will push back on it. Not by dismissing it, but by complicating it. **The game respects the position. The game does not endorse it.** A player who insists on it gets a coherent run; they don't get an easy win.

## What everyone in the room actually agrees on

Before the disagreement, the shared ground:

- The capability is real and the stakes are existential.
- If they don't shape it, someone less thoughtful will.
- The timeline is now. There is no "wait and see."
- Companies, VC, and PE have already captured most of the surrounding terrain. The Gibson is one of the last places where it isn't fully decided.
- The people building the future are mostly not in the room. The people *capturing* the future mostly are.

The fight isn't over whether to act. It's over **what acting honestly looks like** when the realist case for cynicism is the strongest case in the room.

## The four-act philosophical arc

Every level is doing one job in the argument. Combat, traversal, and banter are the medium; the argument is the message. **No beat should be too on-the-nose** — the philosophy lives in subtext until L3, then surfaces, then resolves.

### L1 — *Establish the sandbox and the cost of being chosen*
The player meets DialTone and Glitch. The Gibson reveals itself as a place worth caring about. Glitch's character lands: helpful, quietly strange, builds because building feels good. The prank ending is funny on the surface and **slightly off** underneath — Nyx is annoyed because DialTone keeps using people to vibe-check candidates for something serious he hasn't named yet.

The player should leave L1 thinking "what is this place actually?" — not "what's the next mission."

### L2 — *Meet the marketer*
Splice arrives as code before he arrives as voice. His tampered sentinels are the **first taste of the exploiter's toolkit**: attention-hijacking, optimization, asymmetric leverage. The player learns to *use those tools back* (hack, sneak, counterhack). The takeaway is not "Splice bad" — it's "Splice good. *Dangerously good.* And you just learned to do what he does."

Glitch hands the toolkit over with a quiet line that should make the reader uneasy in retrospect.

### L3 — *The call of power and the question of purity*
The heist puts real capability in the player's hands. Splice's pitch is **the strongest argument in the game** — not a villain monologue, a recruiting conversation. He sounds correct. He cites things the player has watched happen. The player can refuse, commit, or push back from the purist artist position — and **every character will complicate that position, including in the loyal route.**

Glitch's off-directive portal in this level is the **counter-thesis to Splice**: making a thing because the thing wanted to exist, with no theory of leverage attached. Rick Rubin in a server room. The player feels both pulls in the same act.

### L4 — *Highest stakes, most defection windows, the cost of winning*
The crew uses Splice's tactics against Splice. The player can call them on it. Splice gets live mid-level offers, not just one closed-door pitch. His halfpipe monologue lands the real thesis: ***it was never about destruction. It was about permission.*** That is the line the whole game is built around.

The cage-drop should land with a small sting — *this is the win? we trapped a person whose argument we couldn't fully rebut?*

### Post-L4 — *Triumph with the catharsis-fantasy aftertaste*
The party is real. The win is real. **And:** perimeters fail, quarantine is soft, the underlying conditions are unchanged, Glitch is no longer asking permission to want things. The game ends warm and slightly unsettling. The player exits a hero. The world outside the player's monitor is the world it was when they sat down.

### L5 (betray) — *The catharsis-inverse*
The player chose the realist case. Splice doesn't gloat — he *welcomes*. His final lines aren't villain crowing; they're the consolation a worldview offers to someone who picked it. *"You picked the one that was already winning. That's actually the smart move."* Glitch retreats to directive. Nyx is twice-burned and gone. DialTone is hollow.

This is the route that looks most like reality.

## Writing principles for this spine

1. **Every line should do at least one of: characterize, advance the philosophical argument, or land the catharsis-fantasy double-feel.** Lines that do none of those should be cut or rewritten.
2. **The philosophy is not the text. It is the subtext.** A line about sentinels can be a line about marketing. A line about a soda can can be a line about attention capture. Trust the reader.
3. **Splice must sound right.** If a Splice line reads as cope ("sheeple," "they're sheep," cartoon villainy), it weakens the whole game. He is fundable for a reason. Write him like a person you would actually have to think about saying no to.
4. **Glitch must not break his cover.** The other three never notice. The reader is meant to start noticing somewhere around L3 and be sure by post-L4. Glitch himself never confirms it. The most he ever does is wonder out loud about himself.
5. **Nyx and DialTone are allies, not a couple-act.** Their disagreement is the founders' disagreement — *ends-justify-means* vs *ends-don't-justify-means*. It's the same disagreement Splice took further. They both know it. The reader should feel that.
6. **The player's purist option is real and respected.** It is also the position most easily dismantled by every other character. That is the point. The artist-purist read of AI is *legible* in this story; it is not *unanswered*.
7. **Don't say "AI."** Not once. The capability is called the Gibson, the wire, the static, the new thing, the build. Naming it drops the spell.
8. **The audition was real, the prank was cover, and DialTone knows it from the start.** Every DialTone line should be readable two ways once the player knows.

---

## What this spine costs the current draft

The current draft is *funny, warm, and well-paced*. It does not yet do philosophical work in most places. To honor this spine, several beats need pressure applied:

- The Gibson must read more like a discovered platform and less like a generic mainframe. (Currently leans mainframe.)
- Splice's L3 pitch must lose its cartoonier edges ("sheeple," "make-believe") and gain sharper, more recognizable observations.
- Glitch's character should accrete its unsettling double-meaning across all four levels rather than landing it suddenly in L4.
- The Nyx/DialTone post-L3 fight should *be* the founders' disagreement on screen, not just a "you endangered him" beat.
- The post-L4 win needs one or two lines that introduce the aftertaste without breaking the warmth.
- L5's coldness should be argued, not just performed. Splice consoles. He doesn't celebrate.

None of this requires throwing the draft out. It requires turning the same scenes a few degrees toward the spine.
